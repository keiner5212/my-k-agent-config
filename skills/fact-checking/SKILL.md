# Fact-Checking

## When to use
- Verify a factual claim, statistic, quote, image, or timeline.
- Assess whether a claim is current, complete, and accurately framed.
- Produce a transparent verdict backed by direct evidence.

## Hard rules
- Quote the claim exactly and preserve qualifiers, dates, and scope.
- Split compound claims into independently verifiable subclaims.
- Distinguish fact, opinion, prediction, satire, and unsupported inference.
- Prefer authoritative primary sources; never invent or silently infer evidence.
- Verify a source says the claimed thing in its original context.
- Use "Unverifiable" when evidence cannot support a reliable conclusion.

## Workflow
1. Capture the exact claim and identify claimant, date, location, and audience.
2. Break it into atomic claims and mark each as checkable or non-factual.
3. Search official records, original datasets, primary research, and direct statements.
4. Check dates, definitions, numerator, denominator, population, and comparison baseline.
5. Seek independent corroboration for material or disputed claims.
6. Assign one verdict: True, Mostly True, Mixed, Mostly False, False, or Unverifiable.
   True requires direct confirmation; Mostly True has minor qualification; Mixed has
   material true and false parts; Mostly False has a limited true element; False is
   contradicted by reliable evidence; Unverifiable lacks sufficient evidence.
7. Report evidence links, limitations, and what evidence would change the result.

## Checklist
- [ ] Claim is quoted without removing qualifiers.
- [ ] Every material subclaim has direct evidence or an uncertainty statement.
- [ ] Statistics include units, period, denominator, and absolute values where relevant.
- [ ] Images and video have source, date, location, and edit context checked.
- [ ] Current facts are not supported only by outdated evidence.
- [ ] Citations link to the source that establishes each claim.

## Anti-patterns
- Giving a single verdict to a compound claim without checking each part.
- Treating a search snippet, repost, or headline as evidence.
- Confusing relative change with absolute change.
- Calling a claim false solely because evidence is incomplete.
- Assuming intent, motive, or authenticity without supporting evidence.
