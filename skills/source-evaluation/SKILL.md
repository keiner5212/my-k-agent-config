# Source Evaluation

## When to use
- Assess a source before using it for a factual or technical claim.
- Compare conflicting reports, studies, specifications, or statistics.
- Check citation quality and evidence traceability.

## Hard rules
- Use primary sources for direct facts, data, policies, and specifications.
- Use secondary sources for context, synthesis, and discovery only.
- A domain suffix is not proof of quality or independence.
- Verify that cited material supports the precise claim, not a nearby claim.
- Disclose material funding, advocacy, commercial, or selection bias.
- Do not raise confidence through repeated copies of one original source.

## Workflow
1. Identify source type, author, publisher, publication date, and original evidence.
2. Apply CRAAP: currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, and purpose.
3. Trace each material claim to its primary source or underlying dataset.
4. Check methodology, citations, corrections, conflicts, and missing context.
5. Seek independent corroboration for high-impact or disputed claims.
6. Rate source fitness: strong, usable with limits, weak lead, or unsuitable.
7. Report the rating, evidence basis, and remaining uncertainty.

## Checklist
- [ ] Author or organization has relevant expertise and accountability.
- [ ] Date and version fit the claim's time sensitivity.
- [ ] Source states methods, evidence, and meaningful limitations.
- [ ] Citations are direct, accessible, and accurately represented.
- [ ] Purpose and financial or political interests are identified.
- [ ] Independent sources confirm material claims where possible.
- [ ] Primary and secondary claims are labeled correctly.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating popularity, search rank, or a polished site as credibility.
- Citing news, blogs, or summaries instead of original evidence.
- Using an abstract, snippet, or quote without full context.
- Calling sources independent when they share the same study, dataset, or press release.
- Treating anonymous claims as established evidence.
